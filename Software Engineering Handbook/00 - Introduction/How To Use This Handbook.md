# How To Use This Handbook

> **In one line —** read the lesson, close it, then explain it out loud from memory; if you cannot, you have not learned it yet.

---

## The structure

The handbook contains several kinds of notes, and they are read differently.

| Type | Folders | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **Concept essays** | 00, 01 | Read once, slowly. These change *how you think*, not *what you know*. |
| **Technology notes** | 02 – 14 | Reference. Read on demand, answer the 26 questions, do the exercise. |
| **Case studies** | 15 | Read after you know the components. They connect everything together. |
| **Templates** | 16 | Copy them when you start something new. |
| **Your own notes** | 17 | Write here constantly. |

---

## The workflow for one lesson

```text
1. Read the note top to bottom          (~10 minutes)
    ↓
2. Close it and redraw the diagram      (~5 minutes)
    ↓
3. Answer the four final questions out loud
    ↓
4. Write section 25 — Personal Notes
    ↓
5. Do section 26 — Workbook Exercise
    ↓
6. Move on
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Steps 4 and 5 are the ones that actually create memory. Steps 1–3 only create familiarity — and familiarity feels exactly like knowledge while you are reading, then disappears the moment you close the file.

---

## Sections 25 and 26 are intentionally empty

Every technology note ends with two blank sections:

- **25 — Personal Notes:** what you learned, what confused you, links you found, things to research later.
- **26 — Workbook Exercise:** one practical task, left unchecked.

They are blank on purpose. If someone else fills them in for you, the note becomes something you *read* instead of something you *did*.

---

## Reading order

You do not read this handbook front to back.

```text
01 - Foundation              ← read this fully, first
    ↓
02 - Computer Science        ← read this fully; it explains everything below it
    ↓
whatever you are working on  ← from here on, jump freely
```

After Foundation and Computer Science Fundamentals, the handbook becomes a **reference**, not a course:

- Building an API? → folders **06** and **07**
- Debugging a slow page? → folders **08** and **14**
- Adding login? → folder **09**
- Deploying something? → folders **12** and **13**

---

## How to know a lesson is finished

You can answer these four questions without looking:

1. **What is it?**
2. **Why does it exist?**
3. **Where does it belong in the architecture?**
4. **When should I use it — and when should I not?**

> [!TIP]
> If you can also name one alternative and explain why you would *not* choose it, you understand the concept better than most people who use it every day.

---

## Linking notes together

Notes link to each other with relative Markdown links, so they work both on GitHub and in Obsidian. When you notice that two notes connect, **add the link yourself.**

The value of this handbook grows with the number of **connections**, not the number of files.

---

## A warning about speed

> [!CAUTION]
> There are around 200 notes here. Reading all of them in two weeks will leave you with nothing. Two notes a day, each with the exercise actually completed, will give you architecture-level understanding within a few months.

The bottleneck is never the reading. It is the building.
