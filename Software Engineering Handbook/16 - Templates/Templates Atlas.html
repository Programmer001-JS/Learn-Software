Atlas of the Templates

Five documents you copy rather than read: one for learning a technology, one for planning a task, one for recording a decision, one for reviewing a diff, one for securing a system. Sheet 1 is the route a single piece of work travels past all five; every other sheet opens one template and shows its sections, the order you fill them in, and why each one is there. A template is not paperwork — it is a list of the questions people forget to ask until the answer is expensive.

Sheet 1

The Work Line

Transit diagram Covers · Universal Technology Learning Template · Task Template · Architecture Decision Record · Code Review Checklist · Security Checklist

One piece of work, one route, eighteen stops. Each template boards at the exact station where its question is still cheap to answer. Two exits let work skip the templates entirely — and knowing when to take them is what stops the templates from being ignored everywhere else.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and the template that owns it

01A new technology

A tool, protocol, pattern or service you will have to judge. The learning template exists so that "I've read about it" becomes "I can explain where it is the wrong choice".

02The 26 questions

Copy the template, spend 30–60 minutes filling what documentation can answer, and leave 25 and 26 blank until you have used it. In a hurry, answer only the short six. Sheet 2.

03A task arrives

Size it before describing it. Under an hour, just do it; half a day, one line of goal and one of done-when; a few days, the full template; over a week, split it.

04Goal + done when

An outcome for someone, not an activity — "users can find a product by part of its name", not "implement Elasticsearch" — and acceptance criteria a person can verify, with numbers where numbers apply.

05Not in scope

What someone would reasonably assume is included and is not. The section that prevents the "that's not what I meant" conversation, and the one people skip.

06Reversible?

The switch onto the decision branch. If a choice inside the task could be undone in an afternoon, a line in "approach" is enough. If it is expensive to reverse, it needs an ADR.

D1Context

The facts that force the decision, with the numbers: load, growth, team, deadline, budget. And, explicitly, what is unknown — that explains why the choice favours reversibility.

D2Decision

"We will …" — one paragraph, active voice, unambiguous. A reader should be able to act on that sentence alone.

D3Options

The chosen option with its cons, the rejected ones with the specific reason, and always "do nothing". Most of an ADR's value lives in what was turned down.

D4Reversibility

Cost to reverse, what would lock you in, and the triggers that would make you look again. Then the branch rejoins the task at its approach.

07Approach

How, in enough detail that someone can disagree, with the uncertain parts marked and one line per alternative. The place where "Postgres, not Elasticsearch — 40k rows" saves a fortnight.

08Build

The work itself, with the notes section appended as you go. Dated entries, never rewritten, because the assumption that turned out wrong is the useful detail.

09Read your own diff

The author's half of the review: read it first, get CI green, keep it to one purpose, describe why and how it was verified, and flag the parts you are unsure about.

10The review

Size decides whether review is possible; then correctness, security, data, failure, tests, clarity, performance, style — in that order, with severity labels on every comment. Sheet 5.

11Security-shaped?

Changes touching auth, payments, permissions or personal data get a security review, not just a code review. The code checklist's security section points straight at sheet 6.

12How this ships

Feature flag, backward-compatible migration, rollback plan, monitoring. Decided in the task template before the work started, so none of it is improvised at the end.

13Production

The metric or alert named at stop 12 says whether it works. Support, sales, docs and users are told it shipped.

14Notes + progress

The task's append-only log: what happened, what was learned, which criterion was vague and how it was narrowed. The raw material for every later question about why.

15Revisit trigger fires

An ADR's written condition comes true — a volume crosses X per second, a contract demands exact behaviour, or twelve months pass. Without it, a decision becomes permanent by default.

16Supersede, never edit

The old record keeps what was known at the time. A new ADR replaces it and the old one is marked. The pair together is the actual history.

17Personal notes

Every template ends with them: things learned, questions still open, where it surprised you, sections you never use. Familiarity turns into knowledge here.

18Workbook exercise

One practical task per template, unchecked until done: run it, break it, measure it, write the number down. Then the next unfamiliar technology boards at stop 01.

AExit · just do it

Work under an hour skips the task template. Writing it down for a one-hour job trains people to skip the template for the five-day job too.

BExit · split it

A diff over about 500 lines gets "LGTM", which is approval by trust. The most valuable review comment there is sends it back as smaller changes.

SSecurity branch

The checklist also boards off this line: a new service, a new cloud account, an audit response, an incident. Its first ten items fix most real breaches.

Templates scaled wrong get abandoned

Every template in this folder warns against using it for everything: no task sheet for an hour's work, no ADR for a naming convention, no 26 sections for a tool you will touch once. A template applied everywhere is skipped everywhere — the exits on this map are what keep the stations credible.

Sheet 2

The Twenty-Six Questions

Cutaway section Covers · Universal Technology Learning Template

Stops 01 and 02 of sheet 1, opened up. The same twenty-six questions, asked of every technology, so that a new tool becomes a form to fill in rather than a mountain to climb. Six sections carry the load; two cannot be written from documentation at all — and those two are what turn familiarity into knowledge.

Step key — the sections and the order of filling them

01–03What, why, where

A plain definition with no marketing words, the painful situation it was created for, and a diagram of what sits above, below and beside it. If you cannot define it without its own vocabulary, you do not understand it yet.

04–07Mechanism

How it works (enough to reason about load and failure, not an implementation tour), the core vocabulary, the alternatives with a stated default, and the configuration whose default is wrong for production.

08–09In the world

Who uses it and for what, including the case where it is the obvious choice; and what it depends on, including the dependency people forget until it breaks.

10When NOT to use it

"When you need X and can accept Y" — then the conditions where it is a mistake, and the thing people misuse it as. Load-bearing, and the question that separates understanding from familiarity.

11–13Trade-offs, speed, money

Advantages and disadvantages with the one that matters in bold, performance with a number or an order of magnitude, and cost drivers with the line item that surprises people: egress, log ingestion, idle capacity.

14–15Security and failure

The specific, documented, recurring security mistake — not a generic reminder — and failure modes written as symptom → cause, plus whether it degrades gracefully or all at once.

16Mental model

One physical-world analogy developed in a few sentences, including where it breaks down, which is usually the most instructive part. Load-bearing.

17–18Diagram and request flow

The realistic production shape with the surrounding components, then one request traced end to end, again under load, again when something fails. The section that exposes every gap.

19Key takeaway

One sentence you would want to keep if you forgot everything else. Load-bearing — and together with 1, 2, 3, 10 and 16, the whole of the twenty-minute version.

20–22Practice

Common mistakes including one you made, the open-source tools that make daily operation bearable, and learning resources — including the one to skip, and why.

23–24Explanation test, links

Six boxes to tick without notes: two-sentence definition, the problem, the position, one rejected alternative, one misuse, how it fails. Then links, added whenever a connection appears.

25–26Personal notes, workbook

Things learned, open questions, surprises; then something you actually run, break or measure, with a number written down. Left blank until the technology has been used.

F1–F2Copy and name

Copy between the two markers into a new note named after the technology and fill the header: full name, category, architectural layer, sub-topics, three to six related notes.

F3–F4The documentation pass

Thirty to sixty minutes answering what reading can answer, then stop at section 24. Stopping there is the design, not a failure to finish.

F5–F6Use it, then finish

Run the thing, break it, measure it, and only then write personal notes and the workbook exercise from what actually happened.

F7–F8Line one, out loud

Write "In one line" last, capturing what it is and its central trade-off. Then close the note and explain the technology to an imaginary colleague: where you stumble is where the note is weak.

Sheet 3

The Task Sheet

Process plant Covers · Task Template

Stops 03 to 08 and 12 of sheet 1, drawn as a plant. A task goes through a sizing gate, then ten sections in order, and comes out as work someone could disagree with before it is built. The product is not the document — it is thinking forced to happen before the work, where a wrong approach costs fifteen minutes instead of five days.

Step key — the gate and the ten stations

S1Under an hour

Just do it. Writing the task down would cost more than doing it, and filling a template for trivial work teaches the team that the template is ceremony.

S2Half a day

Two lines: the goal and "done when". Enough to know when to stop, with nothing to maintain.

S3A few days

The full template. Fifteen minutes of writing that routinely changes the approach — the whole return on the exercise.

S4More than a week

The full template plus a split. A task nobody can finish in a week is usually two tasks and one unanswered question; find the question and answer it first.

01Title and header

A title that is a verb and an outcome. Status from not started to done, one named owner, a size, a priority with the reason it is now, and links to the issue, ADR or incident.

02Goal

One or two sentences describing an outcome and who gets it. "Users can find a product by typing part of its name" — never the technology you plan to use.

03Why now

The cost of not doing it. If it cannot be articulated, the task may not be worth doing yet, and saying so is a legitimate result.

04Done when

Acceptance criteria that are observable, not aspirational: something a person can see, a number such as p95 under 300 ms, the test that exists afterwards, the runbook updated. Written before code.

05Not in scope

The adjacent task, the deferred nice-to-have, the edge case accepted for now and why. It prevents "that's not what I meant" — and it is the section people skip.

06Approach

Steps or a small diagram, detailed enough to disagree with, with the unsure parts marked for reviewers and one line per alternative. An expensive-to-reverse choice here graduates to an ADR.

07Dependencies and blockers

What you need from which person or team, what must exist first, and which decisions are still open — with the name of whoever decides them.

08Risks

A small table: the thing most likely to go wrong and the thing that would hurt most, each with likelihood and what happens if it does.

09How this ships

Five boxes decided before the work: feature flag or not, migration compatibility and locking, rollback plan and its duration, the metric that shows it working, and who needs to know.

10Notes and progress

Dated entries appended as you go. Never rewrite them: the assumption that turned out to be wrong is where the useful detail lives, and the next similar task starts here.

Sheet 4

The Decision Register

Closed circuit Covers · Architecture Decision Record

The branch at stop 06 of sheet 1, and the return stops 15 and 16. A short, dated, immutable page recording what was decided and why. The decision is usually visible in the code; the context never is — and context is what expires invisibly, so the record exists to say whether a decision is still correct or merely still in place.

Step key — the circuit and the page

C1A choice worth recording

Databases, queues, providers, structural changes, deliberate trade-offs, not doing the obvious thing. The best signal is disagreement: if two competent people argued, the reasoning that settled it is what will be lost.

C2Proposed

Drafted as the next number in docs/adr/ before the decision is made. Written after the fact it becomes documentation, and the reasoning is already gone.

C3Reviewed in a PR

Discussed like code, by the people the decision affects, in the repository beside the system it describes — so the record cannot be separated from what it explains.

C4Accepted

Dated, numbered and immutable. Its value is precisely that it captures the state of knowledge at that moment, including the parts later shown to be wrong.

C5Trigger fires

A condition written in section 07 comes true: a load threshold, a new requirement such as multi-region writes, or the scheduled review date. The question is whether the decision is still correct, not merely still in place.

C6Superseded

A new ADR — 0014 replacing 0009 — carries the new context and links back; the old status changes to "Superseded by". Nothing in the old reasoning is rewritten.

01Title and header

"ADR-00XX — short title stated as a decision", then status, date, deciders who are accountable, who was consulted, and the record it supersedes.

02Context

Two to five paragraphs of facts with numbers: current load, growth, team size, deadline, budget, regulation — and explicitly what nobody knows yet.

03Decision

"We will …" in one unambiguous paragraph. Anyone reading only this sentence should know what to do.

04Options considered

The chosen option with its accepted cons, each rejected option with the specific reason it lost here, and "do nothing / keep the current approach" every time.

05Consequences

Positive, negative and neutral follow-on work. The negative list is where credibility is earned; an ADR with no honest downside is not trusted.

06Reversibility

Cost to reverse — days, weeks, months, effectively permanent — and what would lock you in: data gravity, API contracts, team knowledge. Skipping it gives cheap decisions three weeks and expensive ones an afternoon.

07Revisit triggers

A number, an event, a date. Written while the reasons are fresh; without them every decision is permanent by default.

08Notes

Links to benchmarks, spikes, vendor comparisons, the meeting and the chat thread — anything that counts as evidence for the context above.

Editing an ADR destroys its purpose

A rewritten record always looks correct and therefore teaches nothing. When circumstances change, write the superseding ADR and mark the old one. Also fatal, more quietly: keeping them in a wiki that gets migrated, omitting the rejected options, and writing them for everything, which trains people to ignore them.

Sheet 5

The Review Ladder

Ladder plate Covers · Code Review Checklist

Stops 09 and 10 of sheet 1, opened up. Before any rung is climbed, diff size decides whether a review is even possible; then eight priorities, in order, with human attention spent where machines cannot help. The most valuable thing a reviewer does is refuse to approve a change too large to review — and the least valuable is commenting on formatting in a change that is missing a timeout.

Step key — the gate, the rungs and the comments

D1–D4Diff size

Under 50 lines, real defects found line by line; under 200, good review with some skimming; under 500, structural comments only; over 500, "LGTM" — approval by trust. Ask for a split, or do two labelled passes.

§1Before reading

Purpose statable in one sentence, one change not several, small enough, CI green, and a description that explains why. A change whose purpose is unclear cannot be judged correct.

P1Correctness

Does it do what it claims? Empty, zero, one, null, maximum, negative and duplicate inputs; boundaries; swallowed errors; races on shared state; idempotency under retries; dates and DST; money in integers or decimals.

P2Security

Server-side validation, parameterised SQL, output encoding, object-level authorisation, no secrets or sensitive data in code or logs, and scrutiny for new dependencies and new attack surface. Anything security-shaped goes to sheet 6.

P3Data and migrations

Backward compatible with the previous release, nothing destructive without a plan, table locks sized against traffic, backfills batched, indexes for new query patterns created concurrently, rollback without data loss.

P4Failure behaviour

Timeouts on every external call, a defined user experience when a dependency is slow or down, retries only where safe, partial failures considered, errors observable, and a log line carrying the request id.

P5Tests

The only question that matters is whether a regression would be caught. Tests of behaviour rather than restated implementation, a failure case, nothing flaky, and a pipeline kept under ten minutes.

P6Clarity and design

Could someone change this in a year without the author? Accurate names, comments that explain why, no drifting duplication or premature abstraction, local conventions respected, nothing dead left behind.

P7Performance

Only where it matters and only if measured: queries inside loops, unbounded result sets, loading what could be streamed, cache keys scoped to user or tenant. Speculative optimisation is a cost.

P8Style

A formatter's and a linter's job, run in CI, so that no human ever spends a comment on it and human attention goes where machines cannot help.

§9Operational

Config or environment changes documented, a feature flag for risky or visible behaviour, the metric or alert it needs, API contracts versioned, and the README, runbook or ADR updated.

L1–L5Severity labels

BLOCKING, SUGGESTION, NIT, QUESTION, PRAISE. Labels let a reviewer leave many thoughts of which one blocks, and stop authors arguing about nits as if they were demands.

AThe author's half

Read your own diff before anyone else does, keep it small and single-purpose, say how it was verified, and point at your own weak spots — reviewers will look exactly there.

Approval is not review

The most common failure is approving a diff too large to have read; close behind are style comments on a change missing a timeout, rubber-stamping seniors, reviewing only the diff without opening the surrounding code, missing the migration hidden in a large change, and counting test lines instead of asking whether a regression would be caught.

Sheet 6

The Security Perimeter

Cutaway section Covers · Security Checklist · Code Review Checklist

The branch at stop 11 of sheet 1, and a checklist that also stands alone for a new service, a new cloud account or an audit. The nine sections are drawn as the layers an attacker must cross, with the ten items that cause most real breaches pinned where they belong. Real breaches come overwhelmingly from misconfiguration — a public bucket, a key in a repository — so almost everything on this sheet is a setting you can change this afternoon, before buying any tool.

Step key — layers, the ten, the questions and the bad day

§1Identity and access

MFA on every admin human, a root account with hardware MFA and no keys, roles for services and OIDC for CI, least privilege, no shared accounts, same-day leavers, separate prod accounts, guardrails above IAM, alarmed break-glass credentials.

§2Network exposure

Databases, caches, brokers and dashboards never public; no 0.0.0.0/0 except 80/443 on a load balancer; no inbound SSH; private subnets by default; egress restricted; TLS 1.2+ everywhere with auto-renewed certificates; rate limiting at the edge.

§3Secrets

Not in code, .env files, Dockerfiles or CI logs; a secret manager with an audit trail; push-protected scanning; anything ever committed is rotated, because deleting the commit does nothing. Kubernetes Secrets are base64 and Terraform state is plaintext.

§4Data protection

Encryption at rest and in transit, personal data inventoried, retention enforced, deletion that reaches replicas, backups, caches and vector indexes, no production data in test, passwords hashed with argon2id, scrypt or bcrypt, card data tokenised.

§5Application

Server-side validation, parameterised queries, context-aware output encoding, a CSP without unsafe-inline, object-level authorisation, CSRF protection, HttpOnly Secure SameSite cookies, SSRF allowlists, safe uploads, no stack traces in errors, security headers.

§6Supply chain

Scanning that fails CI on criticals and updates actually applied, lock files committed, base images pinned by digest and rebuilt, CI actions pinned to a SHA, no secrets exposed to fork PRs, signed artefacts verified at deploy.

§7Logging and response

Audit logs on the control plane and the application, shipped to a separate account, with lawful retention; alerts on root use, permission changes and disabled logging that someone reads; a plan that revokes sessions; a SECURITY.md contact route.

§8Backup and recovery

Backups of everything that cannot be regenerated, immutable in a separate account, beyond the reach of the production credential, kept at least 30 days, with keys and runbook stored outside — and a restore performed and timed.

§9People and process

Nobody deploys alone, administrators included in branch protection; security review for auth, payments, permissions and personal data; phishing-resistant MFA; support recovery paths threat-modelled; vendors reviewed, since their breach is yours.

T1–T10The ten

MFA, no long-lived keys, no public buckets, nothing admin-facing on the internet, no secrets in code, undeletable audit logs, tested immutable backups, updated dependencies, parameterised queries and encoding, authorisation on every request.

Q1–Q4Threat model

What an attacker wants, who they realistically are, where the boundary is, and how far they get if they succeed. The fourth is where design effort belongs, because prevention eventually fails somewhere.

R1–R2Contain, preserve

Revoke sessions and credentials and isolate the system without powering it off, then snapshot before rebuilding — the logs and memory are the only record of what happened.

R3–R5Assess, eradicate, recover

Establish what the credential could reach from the audit trail, rotate everything the attacker could have seen, rebuild from infrastructure code, and restore from immutable backups into a properly configured environment.

R6–R7Learn, notify

A blameless review whose finding is never "a person was careless", and notification within legal deadlines you already knew before the incident began.

The cloud provider secures its infrastructure, not your configuration

The recurring mistakes are all on your side of that line: one account for everything, backups and audit logs in the account they protect, secrets "removed" in a later commit instead of rotated, authentication without authorisation, compliance mistaken for security, alerts nobody reads, and a restore never tested — so the recovery time is unknown until the day it matters.
