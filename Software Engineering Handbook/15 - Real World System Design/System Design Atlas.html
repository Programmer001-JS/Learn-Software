Atlas of Real-World Systems

Six systems that everybody cites and almost everybody copies wrongly: a streaming service, an upload pipeline, a ride dispatcher, a shop, a login system and an AI application. Each sheet draws one of them working, with its real numbers attached. The shapes differ, but the same half-dozen decisions keep turning up. Those decisions are worth copying. The service counts are not.

Sheet 1

Two Planes

Cutaway section Covers · Netflix Architecture

Netflix is two systems that share a client and never talk about video. The top half knows who you are and what you may watch. The bottom half holds identical boxes of bytes inside your ISP. The control plane hands out a list of URLs and then steps aside, so a cloud outage makes browsing duller but does not stop anyone who is already watching.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and what it does

01The client

A TV, phone or browser that holds two conversations: small control requests to AWS, and a stream of segment fetches to Open Connect. All adaptation logic lives here, because this is where the network conditions are visible.

02Edge · Zuul

The API gateway authenticates the request and routes it to the nearest healthy AWS region. It is also the switch that makes regional evacuation possible.

03Home page

Rows, artwork, "continue watching" and search suggestions are separate service calls, made in parallel. The page is an assembly of independent answers, each with its own timeout.

04Fallback

The recommendation service is slow, its 80 ms timeout fires, and a generic popular-titles row takes its place. Someone decided this in advance. That decision, not the timeout, is the design.

05EVCache

A memcached-based tier replicated across zones. Most reads never reach a database; Cassandra behind it holds viewing history and user state, multi-region and eventually consistent.

06Playback authorisation

Pressing play asks four questions server-side: is this account entitled, is the title licensed in this country, can this device do the DRM and codec, and is the simultaneous-stream limit reached?

07The manifest

What comes back is not video but a list: segment URLs on the nearest appliance, signed and short-lived, bound to the device, plus DRM licence information. The presigned-URL pattern at planetary scale.

08Start conservative

Segment 1 is fetched at a low bitrate so playback begins in under two seconds. A fast start is worth more than a sharp first frame.

09Step up

The buffer fills and measured bandwidth is good, so the client requests the next segments at a higher bitrate. No server was consulted.

10Drop at once

Someone starts a video call and bandwidth halves. The client sees the buffer draining and drops mid-stream. Quality degrades briefly; playback does not stop. That is the whole purpose of ABR.

11Telemetry

Bitrate changes, stalls, position and completion flow into Kafka, then Spark and Flink, then S3. Billions of events a day feed recommendations, quality-of-experience dashboards and A/B analysis.

12The encoding plant

One studio master is split into shots, encoded in parallel on thousands of machines, with a bitrate ladder tuned per title, producing hundreds of variants across codecs, resolutions, bitrates, audio and subtitles.

13Fill off-peak

Because video is immutable and popularity is predictable, appliances are filled proactively during the ISP's quiet hours. A general CDN caches on a miss; Netflix knows what tomorrow's misses would have been.

14Regional evacuation

When an AWS region degrades, control traffic moves to another region. New plays authorise more slowly; sessions already streaming continue untouched, because their bytes were never in AWS.

Copy the split, not the service count

Netflix runs thousands of microservices because it has thousands of engineers and a platform organisation to make them survivable. With twenty engineers you import every cost and none of the benefit. What transfers at any size: serve immutable bytes from a CDN with signed URLs, put a timeout and a designed fallback on every call, and use strong consistency only for billing and entitlements.

Sheet 2

The Upload Plant

Process plant Covers · Video Processing Platform · Netflix Architecture

One user uploads a 4 GB, 118-minute film, and the plant turns it into six renditions of 240 segments. The user waits seconds and nobody waits for a transcode. Change the payload to documents, images, audio or batch inference and none of the plumbing changes: accept fast, queue, work on a fleet that scales to zero, store immutably, serve from somewhere cheap.

Step key — every stage on this sheet, and what it does

01Ask to upload

The client asks permission, not a place to send bytes. The API checks authentication, quota and plan limits before anything large moves.

02Presigned multipart

The API returns a URL scoped to one object key, expiring in minutes, and creates the job row with status uploading. This is the single most important decision in the system.

03Direct to S3

The browser uploads 400 parts in parallel straight to object storage. When the connection drops at part 250, only part 250 is retried, so the upload resumes on flaky mobile networks.

04ObjectCreated

Completion emits a storage event that wakes the orchestrator. Nothing polls, and the application tier was never in the byte path.

05Probe and validate

ffprobe reads the real container rather than trusting the extension: 118 minutes, 3840×2160, H.264, within plan limits, malware scan clean. A rejected file should cost cents, not compute hours.

06Plan the ladder

Renditions are chosen from the source: 2160p, 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p and audio-only, each in H.264 for compatibility or AV1/HEVC for efficiency, packaged as HLS and DASH.

07Split and queue

The film is cut at keyframes, where segments are independent, into 240 segments. With 6 renditions that makes 1,440 jobs, all queued with a 30-minute visibility timeout.

08Spot worker fleet

Queue depth scales the fleet from 0 to 120 spot instances. Each claims a message, transcodes one segment with FFmpeg in isolation, writes a deterministic key and acknowledges. Bake FFmpeg into the image; installing it at boot slows every cold start.

09Package

Segments are concatenated per rendition, HLS and DASH manifests are generated, and thumbnails and a preview sprite are extracted.

10Ready

Status becomes ready and the uploader is notified. A lifecycle rule moves the original master to cold storage after 30 days; without such rules, storage grows forever.

11Via the CDN

The player fetches a short-lived manifest and long-lived, immutable segments from CDN cache. It starts low, steps up to 1080p, and drops to 480p when bandwidth halves. The bucket is barely touched.

12Job state machine

uploading → uploaded → validating → transcoding → packaging → ready, with failed reachable from each. It lives in a relational database; object storage alone leaves orphaned files nobody can account for.

13Dead letter queue

One segment fails three times on a corrupt region in the source. It goes to the DLQ, an alarm fires, and a human looks; the other 1,439 segments complete. Without a DLQ, jobs sit "processing" forever.

14Visibility timeout

How long a claimed message stays invisible. If it is shorter than the longest job, the queue redelivers work in progress and two workers race on the same output. Here: 30 minutes against a 4-minute worst case.

15Spot capacity

Interruptible, stateless, retryable work is the ideal spot workload: up to ~90% cheaper. On a 2-minute termination notice, stop claiming; the queue redelivers what was not acknowledged.

16Encode against deliver

A slower preset or modern codec costs more compute once and saves bandwidth on every view. Obviously right for a video watched a million times and obviously wrong for one watched twice.

Four ways to build this badly

Transcoding inside a web request, or in a serverless function whose execution limit is a wall. Serving segments straight from object storage and paying full egress. Running FFmpeg on strangers' files without isolation or limits. And building any of it before pricing a managed service, which may cost less than your engineering time.

Sheet 3

The Dispatch Board

Survey map Covers · Uber Architecture

Most consumer systems are read-heavy and cacheable. This one is write-heavy: millions of drivers each report a position every ~4 seconds, and every report is worthless within about ten. Three kinds of data sit side by side here. The map is wiped and redrawn constantly, the trip record is written once, and the cash book must balance exactly however many times a request was retried.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and what it does

01Driver app

Sends its position every ~4 seconds over a persistent connection. Millions of drivers make this a continuous, unavoidable write load: millions of writes per second at peak.

02Gateway

Holds millions of open sockets. New instances get no traffic until clients reconnect, deployments must drain connections in stages, and reconnects must be re-authenticated, not just trusted.

03Location service

Computes the driver's H3 cell and overwrites the entry in an in-memory index (Redis or a purpose-built service). Only the latest value matters for matching, so the hot path gives up durability on purpose.

04Cold path

The same update goes to Kafka, then object storage and batch processing: the full track for fares, pricing disputes, ETAs and analytics. Two paths for one piece of data, each with the guarantee its use needs.

05City → shard

A trip in Lisbon has nothing to do with a trip in Toronto. Geography is the natural partition key: matching, pricing and regulation are city-local, and one city's failure stays local.

06Cell + rings

"Which available drivers are within 3 km?" becomes: my cell, then ring 1, then ring 2, until about 20 candidates are found. Lat/long columns in a relational table cannot answer this at the write rate.

07Rank

Candidates are ordered by ETA, direction of travel, rating, acceptance history, vehicle type and fairness, so the nearest car is not automatically the best one.

08Atomic claim

Compare-and-set on the top driver in Redis, or a conditional update, before the offer goes out. A rider matching at the same instant fails the claim and gets the next candidate. This makes matching a lock, not a search.

09Offer, 15 s

Pushed to the driver's app with a ~15-second timeout. A decline or a timeout releases the claim and the offer moves to candidate 2.

10Accepted

The claim becomes an assignment and the trip moves requested → ACCEPTED as a strongly consistent write. Every step after this is on the strict side of the line.

11Live tracking

Position flows driver → gateway → trip service → rider over persistent connections rather than polling. A second of staleness is invisible and acceptable.

12Completed

Trip state → COMPLETED. The fare comes from the durable track, not the ephemeral index, and it is computed server-side: a client-supplied fare or spoofed GPS is an attack.

13Idempotent charge

The charge carries idempotency key = trip id. The client retries on reconnect, the provider times out, the queue redelivers, and every repeat returns the original result. One charge.

14Ledger

Append-only, double-entry, reconciled against the payment provider. The key prevents the duplicate; the ledger proves afterwards what really happened.

15Surge

Per cell, over a rolling window: open requests against available drivers. A higher multiplier makes some riders defer and draws drivers in, and the imbalance clears within minutes. It is a stream aggregation.

16Node loss

A location node dies and the index for its cells is gone. Drivers report again within 4 seconds and it rebuilds itself. Matching there is degraded for seconds, and nothing was lost.

Location is among the most sensitive data you will ever hold

It reveals home, work, medical visits and relationships. Minimise precision and retention, keep exact tracks only as long as disputes require, show approximate position before a match, and audit every internal location query. Internal misuse of this data has caused real scandals. And for most applications, PostGIS is the correct answer: this architecture exists because of the write rate, not the query.

Sheet 4

The Shop Floor and the Order Book

Transit diagram Covers · Amazon Architecture

Amazon is two paths that should never be designed as one. The read path is about 99% of traffic: a product page assembled from ~30 services, where something is always slow. The write path is the 1% that matters: an order that must never be lost and an item that should never be oversold. The page always renders and the order is written down first. Everything else, including the stock check, is resolved afterwards.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and what it does

01CDN / edge

Images, static assets and the page shell are served from the edge. The cheapest service call is one that never reaches a service.

02Page composition

One product page needs product data, price, availability, reviews, recommendations, images, Q&A, seller info, shipping estimate, personalisation and experiments: dozens of parallel calls under a total budget of ~100–200 ms.

03The timing chart

Recommendations exceed their 40 ms budget and fall back to a generic popular list. Reviews return the rating in time but not the text, which loads asynchronously. The page renders in ~150 ms with two degraded services.

04The arithmetic

Thirty dependencies at 99.9% each, with any failure breaking the page, gives 0.999^30 ≈ 97%. With fallbacks the page essentially always renders, occasionally less good. Something is always slow.

05The mandate

Every team exposes data only through service interfaces: no direct reads of another team's store, no shared memory, no back doors. Each interface became a contract ready to sell externally, which is how AWS happened. Enforceable in a monolith as module boundaries.

06Cart

Cart writes go to a store chosen for availability over consistency, so they succeed even during a partition. A lost cart write is lost revenue.

07Place order

The order is submitted with an idempotency key generated by the client. The customer double-clicks, the retry carries the same key, and exactly one order exists.

08Accepted

The order is durably written, queued and acknowledged with "we're processing your order" before anything else happens. Never lose the order; resolve the details afterwards.

S1Reserve inventory

An atomic conditional decrement: the authoritative decision point for stock. Compensation: release the reservation.

S2Authorise payment

Card data stays in a tokenising payment service, keeping PCI scope minimal. Compensation: void the authorisation. In the drawn failure, it is declined here.

S3Fraud check

Fraud scoring is a first-class step in the saga, not an afterthought. Stolen cards, account takeover and refund fraud dominate a commerce threat model. Compensation: cancel.

S4Tax and shipping

Prices, discounts and taxes are always computed server-side. A client-supplied total is an attack, not an input.

S5Fulfilment

A fulfilment order goes to the warehouse systems. Compensation: cancel the shipment. Sagas with no compensation logic leave orders stuck in intermediate states.

09Confirm

The confirmation email is sent asynchronously once the saga completes. Every order state change is written to an audit trail for disputes and regulators.

10Inventory

Display from cache, reserve atomically, and oversell occasionally when two channels sell the last unit or a warehouse miscounts. Apologise and refund: cheaper than coordinating every warehouse on every page view.

11Dynamo

Availability over consistency, chosen for the cart: a cart edited on two devices during a partition merges, and a deleted item can reappear. The paper is worth reading for the reasoning. DynamoDB and Cassandra descend from it.

12Peak day

Traffic runs at 10× normal on a date known months ahead. Capacity is pre-scaled, caches pre-warmed, and load shedding sacrifices recommendations to protect checkout. The priority is explicit.

13You build it, you run it

Two-pizza teams own services end to end, including on-call and cost. When the author carries the pager, timeouts, fallbacks and useful logs appear without a mandate. Reliability disguised as an ownership policy.

The worst of both models

Splitting into services while keeping a shared database gives you distributed-systems costs with none of the independence. Microservices are what you adopt when organisational coordination becomes the bottleneck, not when the code gets large. A team of five can take most of this sheet's value with no shared tables, a timeout and fallback on every outbound call, and idempotency keys on every write.

Sheet 5

The Door and the Wristband

Closed circuit Covers · Login System Architecture

Checking identity at the door is deliberately slow, about 200 ms of argon2id, and happens once. After that a wristband is checked on every request in about a millisecond. Password verification is the easy 10%. Breaches happen at the other 90%: what a stolen wristband can do, how fast it can be voided, and the reset flow that works as a second, weaker door.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and what it does

01Edge rate limit

Limit per account (brute force on one user), per IP (one source, many users) and globally on /login (distributed stuffing). Prefer exponential backoff and CAPTCHA to hard lockout, which lets anyone lock out anyone.

02Look the user up

If the account does not exist, hash a dummy value anyway. A missing user returning in 5 ms against 200 ms for a real one is an account enumeration oracle.

03argon2id verify

Deliberately slow, tuned to ~100–300 ms on your hardware and revisited every couple of years. Salts are automatic and per password. A fast hash is the wrong tool; slowness is the feature.

04MFA challenge

A TOTP code checked in constant time, with attempts rate limited. A "remember this device" token keeps MFA from being demanded on every login.

05Session issued

A session entry in Redis, and a refresh token stored hashed with a family id, device fingerprint, issue time and last-used time. That record powers "your active sessions" and "log out other devices".

06Two cookies

Access token in an HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookie for 15 minutes; refresh token in a separate HttpOnly cookie for 30 days.

07Audit log

Every authentication event, append-only, somewhere a compromise cannot rewrite: IP, device, method, outcome. Without it an incident cannot be reconstructed.

08Every request

The cookie arrives automatically, a CSRF token is checked on state-changing requests, and the session is validated against Redis in ~1 ms. Never query the primary database per request.

09The handler

Receives an authenticated user id and nothing more. What that user may do is authorisation, a separate concern and domain logic you should own.

10Access expires

After 15 minutes the access token dies on its own. That lifetime is the upper bound on how long a stolen access token is useful.

11/refresh

The refresh cookie's hash is matched, the old token is invalidated, and a new access and refresh pair is issued in the same family. Every refresh rotates.

12Reuse detected

An already-rotated refresh token arrives again. Either the client retried or the token was stolen, and it is treated as theft: the entire family is revoked, both parties re-authenticate, the user is notified.

13Opaque versus JWT

The deciding question is how fast logout must work. For money, health data or admin capability a compromised session must die immediately, which needs server-side state. A revocation list is server-side state wearing a different hat.

14Where the token lives

HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite, __Host- cookies plus CSRF protection for browsers. A token in localStorage turns any XSS into account takeover. Native apps and APIs use the Authorization header.

15Password reset

Identical response whether the account exists or not; a high-entropy token stored only as a hash; single use, 15–60 minutes (30 here); all sessions invalidated on success; confirm email changes at both addresses.

16MFA strength

SMS is vulnerable to SIM swapping, TOTP is the sensible offline default, push suffers approval fatigue, and passkeys are phishing-resistant because the credential is bound to the origin. Recovery codes are shown once and hashed at rest.

17Build or buy

Delegate authentication unless data residency, extreme user counts or genuinely unusual requirements force you not to. "We can do it ourselves" is true and beside the point.

Assume the users table leaks

Design so that a dump is survivable. With argon2id, an exfiltrated table becomes an expensive offline cracking exercise that pays only for the weakest passwords. Users with MFA or passkeys are unaffected, and the audit log shows exactly what was accessed. With a fast hash, the same dump is mass account takeover by the afternoon.

Sheet 6

The Unreliable Dependency

Wiring diagram Covers · AI Application Architecture

An AI application is a normal application with one strange part. That part is slow, expensive and non-deterministic, it is sometimes confidently wrong, and it believes anything written in its context. Teams that struggle usually treated this as a new discipline and skipped the ordinary work: evaluation, cost caps and retrieval measurement. Teams that succeed wire it in like any other risky third-party API.

Step key — every stop on this sheet, and what it does

01You ask

"What is our refund policy for enterprise customers?" arrives with a user, a tenant and a permission set. Everything downstream depends on that identity travelling with it.

02The gate

Authentication, a per-tenant rate limit, and a check of the remaining cost budget. An expensive endpoint is an attack surface; this is where it is bounded.

03Two caches

Exact-match on the prompt hash first, then a semantic cache on embedding similarity. A hit costs milliseconds instead of seconds and nothing instead of tokens. In the drawn flow, both miss.

04Embed

The query becomes a vector in ~20 ms, using the same embedding model as the index. Changing that model means re-indexing, so plan for it.

05Hybrid search

Vector search returns 25 candidates by meaning; BM25 returns 25 by exact term ("enterprise", product codes, error numbers). The ACL filter runs during traversal, never after.

06Rerank

A cross-encoder scores the merged candidates against the question and keeps the top 4 in ~120 ms. Usually the single largest quality improvement available, and almost always worth its latency.

07Assemble

System instructions, 4 passages and summarised conversation history, within a token budget. More context is not better: it costs linearly and dilutes attention.

08The model

Called with a timeout, retries on 429 and 5xx with backoff and jitter, a fallback model, and a circuit breaker. On a bad minute the answer is slightly worse and still arrives.

09Stream

Server-sent events put the first token on screen in 0.7 s while generation continues. Streaming is a product requirement, but validation gets harder because the beginning is already sent, and a mid-stream error comes after a 200 OK.

10Cite or refuse

The answer cites the four passages, and a claim without support is refused. "I don't know" when retrieval found nothing is a feature, and it needs measuring.

11The ledger

Each request is logged with its prompt, retrieved chunk ids, tokens in and out, cost and latency, then written to the semantic cache. Prompts contain user data, so retention has legal implications.

12Ingestion

Documents are extracted, chunked on semantic boundaries with overlap, embedded, and stored with tenant, document id, ACL, headings and version. Source text stays in the relational database; the vector store is a rebuildable index.

13Permission filter

Filtering after retrieval returns nothing or, when forgotten, leaks another tenant's data. One of the most common defects in hastily built RAG. Embeddings can be partially inverted, so protect the store like the source.

14Third-party API

Timeout, retry, fallback, breaker, your own per-tenant rate limit and a hard budget. Provider token-per-minute quotas are shared across your account and are the most common production surprise.

15Prompt injection

The model cannot reliably tell instructions from data. No prompt prevents it. Least-privilege tools, human approval, output treated as untrusted and no secrets in context turn an attempt into a log line.

16Evaluation suite

50–200 real questions with known answers, run in CI. The new model improved faithfulness but refused fewer unanswerable questions, a regression caught before shipping. Evaluate retrieval separately from generation.

17Cost controls

Input tokens are usually the larger share in RAG. Cache, reuse a stable prefix, route by difficulty, trim context, summarise history, and cap per-tenant budgets and agent iterations in code.

Where not to put a model at all

Not where a rule or a SQL query would be correct, instant and free. Not for arithmetic, aggregation or reporting unless it has a tool. Not where being confidently wrong is unacceptable without a human in the loop. Not in an agent loop where a fixed pipeline would do. And never with the vector store as your source of truth.

Sheet 7

What Transfers

Decision tree Covers · Netflix Architecture · Video Processing Platform · Uber Architecture · Amazon Architecture · Login System Architecture · AI Application Architecture

Strip the brand names off sheets 1 to 6 and eight questions remain. Each one was forced on a company by a specific constraint, and each answer is available to a team of five. Ask them in order about your own system. If every answer is no, the right architecture is one database, one deployable, a cache and good timeouts. That is not a failure of ambition.

Step key — the questions, and why each one matters

01Enormous and immutable

The presigned-URL pattern is Netflix's architecture in miniature, available to a team of three: authorise in the application, serve the bytes from S3 and a CDN, and take the largest source of load off the application tier.

02Outlasts a request

Anything over a couple of seconds belongs behind a queue; anything over a minute needs progress reporting and a retry story. The UI must then handle a pending state, which is a real product cost.

03Retry acts twice

In a distributed system, "did it succeed?" is sometimes unanswerable at the moment you ask. Idempotency keys make that ambiguity retryable, and they also block replay of a payment authorisation.

04Spans databases

Separate services with separate databases cannot share a transaction. Sagas trade that for compensation logic and visible intermediate states. Temporal, Cadence or Camunda keep you from hand-rolling them.

05Contended resource

If double-booking is possible it will happen, and to real people. Claim atomically first. Amazon shows the other answer: where a rare oversell is cheap, handle it as a business process instead of a lock.

06Accept or refuse

Strong consistency everywhere kills throughput; eventual everywhere double-charges customers. Decide per dataset by what the business loses in each failure mode. It is usually less strong data than you think.

07Slow dependency

A timeout without a fallback just produces a faster error. For each call, someone must decide in advance what the user sees. That is a design activity, not an infrastructure one.

08Natural key

Sharding is frightening only when every query needs every shard. Uber has geography and SaaS has tenant id. Find the key along which the workload is already independent, and use it early.

09The default

No question answered yes means none of these costs is justified yet. One database, one deployable, a cache and a timeout on every outbound call covers most products. Revisit when a number rules it out.

10Imported costs

Thousands of services, your own CDN, a bespoke geospatial service, Dynamo-style stores, hand-built authentication and a self-run video pipeline all exist because of scale or special constraints. Copy one without the constraint and you pay for it with nothing in return.

Copy the discipline, not the diagram

These architecture diagrams are the most copied in the industry and among the most often mis-copied. Teams take the service count and leave behind the ideas that would have helped them: the two-plane split, a designed fallback per dependency, idempotent writes, ephemeral state kept apart from the durable log, and consistency chosen per dataset. None of those needs a platform team. All of them need someone to ask the question.
